Lead story

Students Become the Technology

HTIL's K–12 program turns brainwaves and muscle signals into a hands-on route into computer science — building the tools, curricula, and competitions that let students read, program, and control their own physiology.
Draft Issue
Fall 2026
Vol. 1 · No. 1
htilua.org

Physiological computing asks a simple but unfamiliar question: what if the computer's input device were your own body? In classrooms across Alabama, students place a sensor on their forearm or slip on an EEG headband and watch a live signal appear on screen — a muscle's electrical activity, a brain's changing rhythms. Then they write the logic that turns that signal into action: a cursor that moves, a robot that walks, a drone that lifts off.

That real-time feedback loop is what makes the work stick. Students are not reading about technology from the outside; for the length of a lesson, they are the sensor. Curiosity about "can I control it?" does the motivational work that worksheets usually have to do.

What started as a research question about brain–computer interfaces has grown into HTIL's largest education effort — a decade of classroom studies, a block-based programming environment used by students as young as 13, a national competition circuit, and an affordable hardware kit designed to put a working biosignal station in a school for the price of a textbook.

This issue is a snapshot of that work: the software, the curriculum, the hardware, the teachers, and what comes next.

Why it matters

A single biosignal activity spans computer science, biology, data science, machine learning, signal processing, and engineering design at once. Few classroom investments cover that many standards from one piece of hardware — and because every student's data is genuinely their own, there is no single right answer to copy.

Physiological computing education is a field HTIL has helped define. Where most outreach programs introduce neuroscience or robotics in isolation, this work sits at the intersection of both — and publishes the classroom evidence alongside the tools.

Software  ·  NeuroBlock

Block-Based Brain–Computer Interfaces, Built for the First Try

NeuroBlock lets a student assemble a working brain–computer interface the way they would assemble a Scratch project — snapping logic, math, and hardware blocks together and running it against a live brain or muscle signal.

NeuroBlock is a visual programming environment in the Scratch tradition, extended so that the signals feeding a student's program come from real sensors rather than a keyboard. Students drag blocks for logic, loops, math, and data processing, then connect them to blocks that read physiological data and drive connected hardware. No prior neuroscience background is required — the environment does the signal handling so the student can focus on the program.

It is deliberately hardware-agnostic at its core. Today NeuroBlock reads EEG from the Muse 2 headband — surfacing Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, and Gamma bands for students to reason about — and muscle activity from the OpenBCI Ganglion. Output blocks control physical devices, including the VEX AIM robot and the Tello drone.

The pedagogical bet is that a competition is the best curriculum. NeuroBlock's flagship event, the Brain Drone Race, asks competitors to out-focus one another: the electrical signals from their brains, not a joystick, steer the drone. It is a format that reads instantly to an audience and has carried the work into mainstream coverage.

Curriculum  ·  K–12

From Clenched Fists to Linear Functions

The lab's curriculum work puts a biosignal in the middle of the math lesson. Students generate the data with their own bodies, then do the math on it — counting, graphing, comparing fractions, fitting functions.

Physiological computing earns its classroom time by making abstract mathematics physical. A student who squeezes a sensor and watches the number rise is doing measurement before they have the word for it. A class that collects five peak values from five clenches and turns them into a bar graph is doing data analysis where every bar has a story. When the same data is used to compute a slope — a peak value divided by the time it took to get there — the function is something the student felt happen.

HTIL has mapped this approach across every grade band, from counting in kindergarten to vectors and conditional probability in high school, aligning each activity to state standards. The progression is deliberate: lower grades work with counting, pattern, and measurement; middle grades with data, ratios, and rate of change; upper grades with model fitting, classification, and inference.

"Every data point is personal. There is no single right answer for a classmate to copy."

PhysioML carries the idea into machine learning. It is a web-based environment where students apply ML concepts to real-time physiological data rather than to a pre-packaged dataset — the same features-and-labels reasoning they meet in a textbook, but grounded in a signal their own body produced.

PhysioBots connects muscle signals directly to robot control, so the payoff of a well-chosen threshold is a machine that moves.

Activities are written to sit alongside — not replace — the worksheets an Alabama classroom already uses. They are the lesson where the data is real.

Hardware  ·  MioPod

A Classroom Biosignal Station for the Price of a Textbook

Commercial EMG equipment runs from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars per unit — a non-starter for most schools. The lab's open ESP32-based design targets a complete station for under $25.

The lab's affordable EMG platform — MioPod — starts from a hard constraint: the hardware has to be cheap enough that a teacher can kit out a classroom without a grant, and simple enough that a student can build and debug it. The design is built around an ESP32 module (roughly $4 in parts at classroom quantities), which supplies the ADC, wireless radio, and processing headroom for a live biosignal dashboard without any accompanying computer beyond a laptop or Chromebook.

On the analog side, the front end follows the classic instrumentation chain: a pre-amplifier, a band-pass filter tuned to the physiological EMG range of roughly 20–500 Hz, a notch filter to reject mains noise, and an envelope detector so a signal that swings positive and negative becomes a reading a student can plot. That chain is not hidden from learners — building and testing it is part of the lesson, and students can see exactly why each stage exists.

From there the ESP32 samples the signal, filters it digitally, and streams it wirelessly to a browser dashboard or to the same block-based environment students use elsewhere. The result is a station that covers signal acquisition, sensor design, filtering, wireless data, and visualization — and that a school can afford to own thirty of.

Why build our own

  • Commercial EMG sensors: $200–$2,000+ per unit
  • MioPod target BOM: ≈$4 in parts
  • Complete station target: under $25
  • Open design — students can inspect and rebuild every stage
  • Wireless by default — no tethered lab bench required

Where it's been tested

The EMG platform has been validated in Alabama's Black Belt schools, and the lab is extending the model with interested partners in Florida, Georgia, Illinois, and the D.C.–Maryland–Virginia region.

Teachers & Outreach

Building the Teacher Pipeline First

A tool a teacher cannot troubleshoot is a tool a teacher abandons. Most of the lab's K–12 effort goes into the people who carry the technology into a classroom — and keep it running.

Biosignals are noisy. A sensor that worked in a workshop can misbehave on a Monday morning with thirty students, and the difference between a program that spreads and one that stalls is whether a teacher can diagnose the problem. HTIL treats that as a design constraint, not an afterthought: professional development, plain-language materials, and support for the first three lessons matter as much as the software itself.

The lab's teacher-facing work runs through a set of funded programs. An NSF RET Site trains Alabama STEM teachers in sensing technologies, bringing educators into the lab to work on research-grade equipment they then take back to their schools. That site is itself the successor to earlier teacher-training work that has reached on the order of two dozen pre- and in-service teachers, alongside a decade of summer camps and neuro-camps that have put physiological computing in front of roughly 800 K–12 students.

The Alabama Power Foundation supports expansion specifically into West Alabama and the Black Belt — the rural and historically underserved communities where a low-cost, self-contained station has the most to offer, and where NSF broadening-participation priorities point. The lab's NSF CAREER award takes the same question directly: how do students in rural schools engage with physiological interfaces when the technology comes to them?

What teachers get

  • A start-up path that does not assume a neuroscience background
  • Lesson sequences with a clear first, second, and third lesson
  • Guidance on the failure modes that actually happen in class — bad contact, motion artifact, mains noise
  • Hardware students can see into, so a fault is a puzzle rather than a mystery
  • Curriculum mapped to state standards, so the lesson earns its slot in the year

The un-failable first lesson

The lab's classroom design assumes the first session must succeed for every student, not just the confident ones. The recommended opener is a mirror, not a challenge: put the sensor on, watch your own signal move, and see how blinking, clenching, or relaxing changes the trace. Nothing to get right, nothing to fail — but by the end of the lesson every student understands what a biosignal is, from the inside.

Impact & Next

What's Next for K–12 at HTIL

The lab's education work is entering a phase where the question shifts from "does this work in a classroom?" to "how does it reach every classroom that wants it?"

Three things are converging. Consumer-grade brain and muscle sensors have dropped below the price point where a school can reasonably buy them. Funding agencies are actively prioritizing broadening participation in computing. And public familiarity with neurotechnology — for better and worse — has never been higher, which makes the classroom conversation easier to start and more important to get right.

HTIL's position is unusual: the research, the software, the hardware, and the competition format all sit in one lab, and all of it has already been in front of students. That makes the next steps concrete rather than speculative — widening the platforms the software runs on, formalizing the curriculum and teacher certification around it, and turning the Brain Drone Race from an event into a competition circuit that schools can enter the way they enter a robotics league.

It also brings responsibilities. Recording physiological data from minors raises real privacy and ethics questions, and the lab's approach is to treat those as design requirements from the start — keeping data handling transparent, minimizing what leaves a student's device, and framing the whole enterprise as physiological literacy: learning how your own body works, rather than upgrading it.
